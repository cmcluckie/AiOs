AiOS, part 5.  "Pointers were my nightmare in computer science.  So naturally, a pointer is what broke."...

Confession.  Of everything in computer science, pointers were always the thing that scared me.  A variable that holds the address of something else, and if that address is wrong you quietly write over whatever happens to live there.  I got through it in school.  I never really made peace with it.  So when the next rung on the ladder was memory, that's exactly where I wanted to go.  I wanted to actually understand how a program gets memory, where it comes from, and what's going on with those addresses.

I asked the AI to find a common university problem that needs a lot of memory.  It came back with Conway's Game of Life.  I'll be honest: I'd never heard of it, never played it, and don't really care about it.  Apparently it's a classic.  What I cared about is that it needs two 24 KB grids and only gets 4 KB of stack.  No way to fake it.  It has to ask the operating system for memory, and until this week the operating system had no idea how to say yes.

So the loader grew up a little.  When the machine boots, the firmware leaves a map of memory behind: here's RAM, here's reserved, here's mine.  The loader had been ignoring it.  Now it reads it, finds the chunk it's sitting in, and hands out pieces.  You ask for memory, you get back a pointer: an address that says "your bit starts here."  It never takes memory back, because nothing's asked it to.  It doesn't protect anything, because there's nobody to protect it from.  Thirty-five lines, and a third entry on the door: write, exit, and now alloc.

And then my nightmare showed up, right on schedule.  The first run printed nothing.  The program kept a pointer saying where its grid was, and the very first chunk of memory the loader handed out landed right on top of it.  So the program wrote its grid over its own note about where its grid was, and wandered off into the weeds.  The textbook pointer bug, live, right in front of me.  The cause: programs here have no way to say "and I need a little room for my own variables," so the loader thought that space was free.  One line to fix.  Hello world had the same bug the whole time and never noticed, because nobody else wanted that memory.

I understand pointers better after one evening of this than after a whole semester.  If they scared you too, open the commit below and read the 35 lines.  They're a lot less scary when you can see exactly where the memory comes from.

The test that can fail on its own: ask for 128 MB on a 64 MB machine.  It says no and keeps going.  And the ladder held: hello world and Hanoi run on the new loader without a single change.
