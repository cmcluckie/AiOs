- Chris's notes for part 18 (the gate), 2026-10-17: a story for the opening.  He became a welder instead of a computer guy; around the time he got into programming, the mother of the person he was dating gave him an old IBM 5150.  After about three weeks he pulled it apart to see how it all connected, and went looking for the processor.  It was an 8088 (he thought), and he found it odd that it was a long rectangle, not the square Pentiums he'd seen.  The family's own computer was an IBM, likely an Aptiva (confirm), with a sound card that did its work in software; he spent a lot of time on it playing Myst, and the family were nearly adopted parents.  Later he read books on the 286 and 386 and on protected mode, and nobody explained it well.  Several of the articles he read back then said the 386 had protected mode; he does not know why.  The line he wants in the article, in his own words: from what he read back then, the 386 was the one that added protected mode, but in writing this part he learned the 286 added it, and it sucked so much that no one used it.  What he learned writing this part: the kernel starts a program and tells the processor it runs with less power, so it can't touch kernel memory, the screen or the ports; instead of calling those directly, the program puts a message on a bus and the kernel picks it up, does it, and returns the answer (in brackets, for the exact version: the program runs a trap instruction, `int 0x80`, the processor jumps to a fixed place in the kernel with the arguments in registers, and the kernel returns).  He wonders why school glossed over something that simple.  Facts checked 2026-10-17: the 5150 is the 1981 IBM PC with an 8088, which is 16 bits inside and 8 on the outside bus, a 40-pin chip in two long rows; the 286 (1982) had protected mode and four rings, 0 to 3; the 386 (1985) added the 32-bit version, paging and a mode for old DOS programs; programs run in ring 3 and the kernel in ring 0; the 8088 has no protected mode at all, so the 5150 on his desk ran every program with full power, as AiOs does today.  Why the press credited the 386 is not known, and Chris is leaving it as his own doubt, not researching it; a lead, not checked: the 286's protected mode was close to unusable (64 KB pieces, no way back to real mode without a reset, old programs wouldn't run), so the 386 was where it first worked for ordinary software.
