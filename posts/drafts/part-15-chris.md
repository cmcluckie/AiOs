# Part 15: Chris's notes, as he said them (2026-10-14)

Something that I keep on talking about with all of my teams is disconnecting from the matrix. And it's a bit of a weird concept because I don't think everybody really gets what I mean by disconnecting from the matrix. And let's put it this way. A lot of times when we're doing traditional software development is that we take a lot of documentation for granted because we don't really need to write down a lot of the things, especially when we're in small teams, we don't need to write down a lot of the things that we take for granted because really we know that it's inside of our head and we actually start implementing and writing the code is we'll remember it and we'll put it inside of the code. Now, when we start talking about agentic development, this is where things become really difficult because the problem is, is agentic can't read our minds. And sometimes we're so used to working in the traditional ways that we work, we forget that it can't read our minds, and we have this expectation that it's somehow connected to our brains. And so when I say disconnected from the matrix, it's kind of what I'm talking about. I kind of look at Neo unplugging from the matrix and having the AI know exactly what it wants it to do means that you have to leave it notes and you have to leave it clues. And that's where when you're working in a 100% agentic team, you need everybody to be documenting everything. But you also don't want to be documenting everything so intrinsically that you lose the benefits of AI and its ability to be able to figure things out. And this is one of the biggest challenges that I've found, is striking that balance inside of AI.

And where I start to get really kind of challenged inside of this is for this next post, is when we start talking about AI coding and AI agents, and not only having the tests that need to happen as part of making sure that the code does what it's expected to do, but also making sure that the AI is doing what we expect it to do. So just because a requirement's met by a test doesn't necessarily mean that the AI did what you think that it should have done. So for example, we have a software framework that we use internally for UI controls. And from time to time, what we'll find is that the AI won't use those software controls because it doesn't meet the requirements of what it needs 100%. And so instead of asking the question back to the human, hey, what can we do to be able to solve this? It just goes and solves it on its own and will write like a new control or do something completely different, right? How do you write tests around that? We can't. And that's the problem. You can't write tests around that. The only thing that you can do is have a decision chain and a list of steps that the AI had and extract from that.

And so almost kind of the way that I look at it is you have a director and a builder. You have two AI agents open where you have a director that takes specifications and then a builder that actually builds those specifications. But the job of the director is to take those specifications that a human wrote, break those down into such small segments that they can be decided upon inside of a decision log and given to a builder. And the builder goes and builds that thing, and something that isn't AI, that's deterministic, can actually go and verify that that is actually what's built. And that's kind of where this post comes from, is that ability to be able to go and verify in a system that is meant to be able to just solve a problem. How do you make sure that it solves the problem the right way?
