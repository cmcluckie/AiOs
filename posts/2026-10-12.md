AiOS, part 2.  "Linux can add forever.  It can never take anything back out."...

Part 1 was the fun bit: it booted, fourteen bytes, done.  This is the boring bit that made it possible, and I promise it's less boring than it sounds.

Before I let the AI write a single line of code, we wrote a rulebook.  A constitution, if you want to be dramatic about it, and I do.  The first eight commits in the repo are all rules.  The ninth is the first line of C.  Anyone who's worked with me knows I'll argue about the process before I'll touch the thing.

Ten rules.  No vote can change them.  In plain English:

1. No purpose, no operating system.  Everything in the machine has a written reason for being there.  "It came with the install" is not a reason.

2. One door.  Programs, drivers, tests, the loader itself, everything comes in the same way: a request that says what it needs and why.

3. Source only.  Nothing pre-compiled.  If I can't read it, it doesn't get in.

4. Reviewed before entry.  Against this machine's purpose, every time.  And review means "someone looked", never "proven safe".  Nobody gets to say proven safe.

5. No waiting on anyone.  Once the code is in, it's ours.  Upstream can send suggestions like everyone else.

6. The core provides the levers and never decides when to pull them.

7. The smart thing and the boss are never the same thing.  The AI proposes.  A deliberately dumb referee decides, by checking boxes it can't be talked out of.  You can't sweet-talk a script that doesn't understand English.

8. Every test is written to fail.  A test that can't go red is decoration.

9. Nobody's confidence counts.  Not the program's, not the AI's, not mine.  Only the record.

10. Everything expires.  Nobody uses it anymore?  It goes.

Here's a question I keep asking myself.  Why is there a firewall?  A firewall is a guard standing in front of a door, checking everyone who tries to come through.  Fine.  But why is the door there in the first place?  If nothing on the machine asked to listen on that port, why does the port exist at all?  On this thing it doesn't.  Nothing listens unless a petition asked it to, with a reason, and the machine we boot doesn't even have a network card.  Not blocked.  Not there.  You can't attack a door that was never built.

Number ten is the whole game.  Linux is an amazing piece of software and it has one rule it has never broken in thirty years: once something's in, it stays in.  That's how you end up with tens of millions of lines and no way to say which ones your machine actually needs.  Same parts here.  Opposite rule.
