# Findings 0013: capabilities as labels, and why SELinux was too hard

Source: a conversation, 2026-10-13, not a petition.  Chris's idea.
Goes to: humans who own the constitution.

1. **The idea.**  Like the attributes on a file, every component carries a
   label: the capabilities it may use.  A separate spec says what each
   capability allows.  The petition asks for them, the referee checks before
   the build that the component does only what its label allows, and the
   nucleus enforces the label while it runs.

2. **It has history.**  Unix permission bits (1971) and Multics access lists
   before them.  Closest of all, SELinux (NSA, 2000): every file and process
   carries a security label, stored as a file system extended attribute; a
   separate policy says what each label may do; the kernel enforces it and
   logs every denial.  AiOS already has the pieces under its own names: the
   capabilities (CAP) are the labels, the capability entry and its fence
   tests are the policy, the petition is the request.

3. **Why SELinux was so often switched off.**  Not the idea: the work.  The
   policy grew to thousands of rules that people had to write and keep up by
   hand.  For years the common advice was to turn it off.

4. **What changes here.**  The tedious part, reading what a component
   actually touches and writing its label, is the quartermaster's job, an
   agent's.  The parts that must stay human stay human: every label the
   quartermaster picks goes in the decision ledger (plan 3.6), granting a
   new capability is on the human-only list, the referee checks labels
   against the code, the nucleus enforces them (fully only once programs
   lose ring 0, plan 9.13), and the tests prove the enforcement holds.

5. **Keep the vocabulary small.**  SELinux's lesson is that the label set
   itself becomes the burden.  The rule stands: capabilities are a small,
   finite vocabulary, and a new one costs a human decision.

6. **For the series.**  The best security idea in Linux, given up because it
   was too much work for people, made practical by agents, with people still
   holding the decisions that matter.
